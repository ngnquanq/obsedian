# Regression Loss Functions

Before a model can learn, it needs a way to measure how wrong its predictions are. The loss function is that measure — a single number that turns the gap between prediction and ground truth into a signal for optimization. The question is which measure to use, and why MSE became the default despite alternatives that seem equally reasonable.

Read [[0. Why Loss function]] first for the general framing of what loss functions do.

## The L^p Norm Family

All common regression losses are special cases of the L^p norm applied to the residual `e = y - ŷ`:

$$
L_p = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |e_i|^p
$$

- **p = 1** → Mean Absolute Error (MAE)
- **p = 2** → Mean Squared Error (MSE)
- **p = 10, 12, ...** → Higher-order losses

The choice of `p` controls three things simultaneously: how differentiable the loss is, how sensitive it is to outliers, and what statistical estimator the minimizer corresponds to.

## MSE (L²)

$$
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$

### Why MSE is the default

**1. Differentiability everywhere**

The gradient of MSE is `2(ŷ - y)` — smooth, continuous, defined at every point. Gradient descent works cleanly. MAE has a kink at zero and is not differentiable there, requiring subgradient methods or approximations.

**2. Statistical grounding: Gaussian MLE**

If you assume prediction errors are i.i.d. Gaussian — the most common noise model in practice — then minimizing MSE is exactly equivalent to maximizing the likelihood of the data. This is not just computational convenience; it is the theoretically correct objective under that assumption.

The Gauss-Markov theorem extends this: among all linear unbiased estimators, the OLS estimator (which minimizes MSE) has the lowest variance. It is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE).

**3. Strict convexity with a unique minimum**

MSE is strictly convex, meaning it has exactly one global minimum. MAE is convex but not strictly so — it can have a flat region with infinitely many minima, making the solution less stable. Strict convexity also means gradient descent converges to the same point regardless of initialization.

> [!warning] MSE is sensitive to outliers
> Squaring the error amplifies large residuals quadratically. A point 10 units away contributes 100 to the loss; a point 1 unit away contributes 1. One extreme outlier can dominate the entire loss and pull the model significantly. This is a cost, not a benefit of MSE.

## MAE (L¹)

$$
\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} |y_i - \hat{y}_i|
$$

MAE treats all errors linearly. An outlier 10 units away contributes 10, not 100. This makes MAE the robust choice when outliers are real signal, not noise — or when the cost of large errors should not be amplified.

**Statistical grounding:** MAE minimizer is the conditional median `median[Y | X]`, corresponding to MLE under Laplacian (double-exponential) noise. If your residuals have heavy tails, MAE is the correct loss, not MSE.

**Tradeoffs vs MSE:**

| Property | MSE (L²) | MAE (L¹) |
|---|---|---|
| Differentiable everywhere | Yes | No (kink at 0) |
| Outlier sensitivity | High — quadratic amplification | Low — linear |
| Unique minimum | Always | Not always |
| Statistical model | Gaussian noise | Laplacian noise |
| Gradient magnitude | Scales with error size | Constant (±1) |
| Optimization | Standard gradient descent | Subgradient or smooth approximation needed |

> [!tip] Gradient magnitude matters for learning rate tuning
> MSE gradients shrink as predictions improve (since `2(ŷ - y) → 0`). MAE gradients stay constant at ±1 regardless of how close the prediction is, which can cause oscillation near the minimum.

## Why Not Higher-Order Losses (L¹⁰, L¹²)?

Higher-order norms are differentiable and strictly convex — both good properties — but they fail in two ways:

**Extreme outlier sensitivity.** A residual of 2 contributes `2^10 = 1024` to an L¹⁰ loss. One outlier can numerically dominate the entire training signal, making the model ignore all other data points.

**Numerical instability.** Gradients scale as `p * e^(p-1)`. For p = 12 and a residual of 5, the gradient is `12 * 5^11 ≈ 58 million`. This causes exploding gradients and requires extremely careful learning rate tuning that does not generalize.

**No statistical justification.** MSE corresponds to Gaussian MLE. MAE corresponds to Laplacian MLE. L^10 corresponds to no standard noise model. There is no principled reason to use it.

## When to Use Which

| Situation | Recommended loss |
|---|---|
| Errors are approximately Gaussian, outliers are noise | MSE |
| Outliers are real data, not measurement error | MAE |
| You need a closed-form solution (e.g., OLS) | MSE |
| Median is a better target than mean | MAE |
| Working with neural networks, standard gradient descent | MSE (differentiability advantage) |
